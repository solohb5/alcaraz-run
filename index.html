Learn more — what the parade is
"Moors and Christians" parades re-enact the medieval centuries when Spain was split between Muslim and Christian kingdoms. Costumed "armies" march in slow formation — embroidered robes, armor, drums, brass bands — and the point is spectacle, not sides: the Moorish companies are usually the most lavish and the most coveted to join. Murcia's version caps the city's September fair, whose heart is carrying the Virgin of Fuensanta down from her mountain sanctuary into the cathedral. It's the whole city in the street on its proudest night, and you arrive right into the middle of it.

Ending A — Madrid, built around a derby · my pick
Sunday: train up, derby at nightSun 20
Atlético Madrid against Real Madrid — the two giants of the city in one stadium. It happens to fall the day after camp ends, and it is the last soccer in Spain before the league takes a two-week break. Tickets are the catch: face value is €40 to €150, but they sell to club members first, so realistically you pay resale prices of about €100 to €250 each.
Learn more — why this rivalry burns
The derby is a class story. Real Madrid is the royal club — the establishment, the galácticos, the most decorated team in Europe. Atlético is the working-class club from the south of the city: harder, louder, forever the underdog that refuses to die, shaped for over a decade by coach Diego Simeone, who turned defiance into a playing style. The Metropolitano holds 70,000, and on derby night it is one of the loudest rooms in European sport. Even people who don't follow soccer circle this fixture.
Monday: Madrid, slowlyMon 21
Madrid's two great museums are free in the evening. The Prado (Spain's version of the Louvre) is free from 6 to 8. The Reina Sofía, home of Picasso's Guernica, is free from 7 to 9. At night there is Sésamo, a little cave bar from the 1950s in the old writers' quarter. Bring cash. The whole day costs almost nothing.
Learn more — the cave bar
Sésamo opened in 1950 as a cellar bar for writers, painters, and students, and it never changed. The walls are covered in hand-painted quotes from the poets who drank there, the house sangria comes by the pitcher, and a pianist plays from about nine most nights. Legend ties Hemingway to the room — the quote painted over the bar is his. It's small, cheap, and feels like a secret even though it sits two streets from Plaza Santa Ana, at Calle Príncipe 7.
Tuesday: fly homeTue 22
Target flight home. If you end up staying to Wednesday, one of Spain's most loved pop bands, La Oreja de Van Gogh, plays their reunion show in the arena that Tuesday night. Tickets run about €40 to €80.

Why I lean A: it removes risk instead of adding it. No ticket deadline, no extra bus, and you end up exactly where the plane leaves. A derby night after a week of tennis camp is a story you tell for years.

Ending B — Granada and the Alhambra
The catch: this ending is on a timernow → late Aug
The Alhambra's famous palace section sells timed tickets that run out four to eight weeks ahead. For your dates, the buying window is right now through late August. Pick this ending fast or it takes itself off the table. Two more honest notes: Granada has no festivals during your days there, and the special nighttime palace visits only run Friday and Saturday, which are the wrong days for you.
Sunday: bus over, evening up in the old quarterSun 20
The direct bus from Murcia takes about three hours. The Albaicín is Granada's old Moorish hill neighborhood — whitewashed lanes and one famous lookout with the sunset view of the Alhambra against the mountains.
Monday: the Alhambra, then flamenco in a caveMon 21
The Alhambra is a thousand-year-old Moorish palace and fortress, and probably the single greatest sight in Spain. Entry to the palace section is about €20 with a pre-booked time slot. That night you see flamenco in the Sacromonte caves, where Roma families have run cave-house shows for generations. The real ones cost €25 to €60. This is the deeper-Spain ending — if Dan is a history-and-culture guy, it wins.
Learn more — the last kingdom and the caves
The Alhambra was the palace of the Nasrids, the last Muslim dynasty in Spain, who held Granada for 250 years after the rest of the country had fallen. It's a fortress outside and a paradise inside — carved plaster like lace, courtyards built around running water, ceilings designed to look like heaven. In 1492 the last sultan, Boabdil, handed over the keys and, the story goes, wept on the mountain pass as he left. The timed tickets exist because millions visit and the rooms physically can't hold them. Zambra — the flamenco you'd see that night — is the Sacromonte cave community's own style, descended from Roma wedding dances, performed at arm's length in rooms carved into the hillside.
Tuesday: up to Madrid, fly outTue 22
A four-hour train puts you in Madrid for the flight, either Tuesday night or at dawn Wednesday. It works, but it is the version with more moving parts.

Dan's coast stop · Sept 7–9 · the research changed the plan
Dan works Monday to Wednesday evenings, so his coast stop needs two things above all: quiet, and good wifi. We checked the calendars, and the two towns this board used to suggest both fail on his exact dates. Sitges is mid Bears Week, its biggest party of the year, September 4 to 13. Peñíscola runs its patron-saint festival September 7 to 17, with open-air concerts right where the good hotels sit. Both are out for the work nights. The new pick is Altafulla — a small beach village with its own train station, 45 minutes from Barcelona, next door to Tarragona's Roman old town.
Hotel Oreneta, Altafulla the pick~€65–75
A small family hotel on the hillside above the village, a short walk from the old town and the beach. Reviewers say exactly what Dan needs to hear: no cars, no noise, very quiet — and it scores 8.8 from over 1,400 guests. Garden terrace, wifi included, honest price. This is the work-week base.
Gran Claustre, Altafulla the splurge~€116–140
A boutique hotel built into a historic building next to Altafulla's little castle, with a rooftop hot tub and a restaurant people rave about. If Dan wants one memorable night out of his quiet stretch, this is it.
Why not Sitges or Peñíscolacut

For sleeping and working, they are out. If Dan skips the coast stop entirely, riding straight through to València early also works fine.

Learn more — what a corrida actually is
Six bulls, three matadors, two bulls each, about twenty minutes per bull. Each fight runs in three acts: the opening capework and the picador's lance, then the banderillas, then the matador alone with the small red cape and the sword. The crowd judges everything — a great performance earns waving white handkerchiefs and, if the president agrees, an ear. It is intense, formal, and bloody. Some people find it unforgettable, some find it unwatchable, and both reactions are normal — you can leave whenever you want. "Sol" means sun seats and "sombra" means shade; shade costs more because the September evening sun is no joke.
Cartagena's Romans festival in the plan · Fri 18free
Cartagena is the old port city thirty minutes from camp, founded by Carthage over two thousand years ago. Every September the whole town splits into Roman and Carthaginian armies and re-fights that history for ten days — torchlit parades, staged battles, and feast camps you can walk through. Almost no foreign tourists know it exists. It opens on your last two camp nights.
Learn more — the real history underneath
Cartagena was founded around 227 BC as Qart Hadast — "New City" — the Carthaginian capital in Spain, and Hannibal launched his march on Rome from this harbor, elephants and all. Rome's general Scipio took the city in 209 BC in one of the boldest raids of the ancient world. The festival re-stages all of it: the founding, Hannibal's wedding, the Roman landing, the battles. Locals join "troops and legions" the way other towns join sports clubs, and the feast camps in the middle of town are open to anyone who wanders in.
The Roman theater in Cartagena Álvaro says goTue free 6–8pm · else €7
A two-thousand-year-old Roman theater that sat forgotten underneath the city until 1988. It is one of Spain's great archaeological wows. Every Tuesday the last two hours are free. It pairs naturally with a Cartagena dinner.
Learn more — the city that forgot its theater
Rome built this 7,000-seat theater around 5 BC under Emperor Augustus. As the city grew, the theater vanished completely — a market, a fishermen's quarter, and eventually a cathedral were built literally on top of it. Nobody knew it existed until 1988, when construction work hit carved stone. The excavation took twenty years, and the museum route now walks you from street level down through the layers of the city and out into the restored theater. One of Spain's great archaeological surprises.

Eat & drink
Casa Carmela — the one big meal in the plan · Sat 12€50 each
A paella house on the beach that has been cooking over orange-wood fires since 1922. Paella was invented in this city, and this is the best version of it. It is lunch only, and you phone in your paella order a day ahead — that is the tradition. Everything else on this trip is cheap so this one meal can happen. It books out weeks ahead. The cheap fallback is a local rice house at €20 to €25.
Learn more — why this place
Casa Carmela opened in 1922 as a beach shack where families changed into swimsuits and someone's grandmother cooked rice. A century later the same family still cooks every paella over burning orange wood from the orchards outside the city — the orthodox way, and the reason for a smoke flavor a gas burner can't fake. The thing to fight over is the socarrat: the toasted, caramelized crust of rice at the bottom of the pan. And note that paella here is lunch, never dinner — Valencians consider evening paella a tourist tell.
The Albufera — where paella comes from in the plan · Sat 12€20–30 each
A big lagoon thirty minutes south of the city, surrounded by rice fields. You take a €5 flat-bottom boat ride from the fishing village, then eat the local eel-and-potato stew for lunch. The tour companies sell this exact trip for €40 to €65. Doing it yourself costs a third of that, and sunset over the lagoon is the best free show in València.
Learn more — why paella starts here
This lagoon is why paella exists. The rice paddies around it have been farmed since the Moors engineered the irrigation a thousand years ago, and paella began as the field workers' lunch — rice, rabbit, snails, and beans cooked flat over a fire. El Palmar was a village of eel fishermen, which is why its own dish is all i pebre: eel in garlic and paprika. The flat-bottomed boats you ride were their working boats. Sunset here, with the rice fields going gold, is the oldest landscape in the region.
Breakfast at Central Bar in the plan · Fri 11about €10
València has a sacred mid-morning meal: a big filled roll with a drink and olives. The best place to have it is a counter inside the city's beautiful old market hall, run by a Michelin-starred chef. A Michelin chef's sandwich in a hundred-year-old market for ten euros. Go at 9:30 when it opens, because the line builds fast.
Caldero at the Cabo de Palos port in the plan · Wed 16€20–35 est
Caldero is the local fishermen's rice, cooked in a rich broth made from the catch. The two port restaurants that do it right are La Tana and Bocana de Palos, both on the little harbor thirty minutes from camp. Walk out to the lighthouse for sunset first, then eat. This is the camp region's best food night.

Learn more — pirates, then fishermen
For centuries this island was a hideout for Barbary pirates raiding the coast. King Carlos III walled it in the 1770s and settled it with Genoese fishing families he had ransomed from captivity in Tunisia — their surnames are still on the doors. It became Spain's first protected marine reserve in 1986, which is why the water is so clear. The island dish is caldero: rice cooked in a rich fish broth the fishermen made from the catch they couldn't sell.

The safety rules
The camp is untouchable
Latest safe arrival in the region is September 12. The retry day at the end stays empty. Any idea that threatens either edge dies, no matter how fun it sounds.
September storms are real on this coast
This exact coast gets sudden violent September storms — locals call them the gota fría. In 2019 one flooded Murcia on September 12 to 14, these literal calendar dates. Europe started fingerprint-and-photo entry checks in 2026. Your first landing in Europe can take noticeably longer than it used to. Leave generous time on the first connection.
Booking rules that save pain
Book Spanish trains through the Trainline or Omio apps, because the official site rejects most US cards. The train to the camp region has only about three departures a day, so book it early and flexible. And Spanish rail workers have gone on strike several times this year with a few weeks' notice — we recheck before travel.
